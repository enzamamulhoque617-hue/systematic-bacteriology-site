1. Morphology

  • Staphylococci are Gram-positive cocci.
  • They are spherical in shape.
  • The cells are characteristically arranged in irregular grape-like clusters.
  • They are non-motile.
  • They do not produce spores.
  • Some pathogenic staphylococci may possess a capsule or slime layer.
Remember: Staphylococci → Gram-positive cocci → grape-like clusters.

2. Important Cell Components

Cell wall

The cell wall provides shape and structural support to the bacterial cell.

Capsule

The notes describe the capsule as a layer outside the cell wall. It can contribute to virulence and can help the organism resist phagocytosis.

Pili

Pili are associated with attachment of bacteria to surfaces.

Flagella

The notes distinguish flagella as structures associated with motility; staphylococci themselves are non-motile.

DNA

DNA contains the genetic material of the bacterium.

Ribosomes

Ribosomes are involved in protein synthesis.

3. Cultural Characteristics

The PDF notes describe staphylococci as organisms that can grow on commonly used laboratory media.

Temperature & pH

  • Growth is described around 35–37°C.
  • The notes give a pH range of approximately 7.2–7.6.
  • They are described as facultative anaerobes.

Nutrient agar

On nutrient agar, colonies are described as relatively large, round, smooth and opaque.

Blood agar

Colonies may show pigmentation and haemolysis. The notes describe typical golden-yellow pigment associated with staphylococcal colonies.

Liquid media

Growth can produce visible turbidity in liquid media.

Selective medium

Mannitol Salt Agar (MSA)
The notes mention mannitol salt agar as a selective medium useful for isolating staphylococci, particularly S. aureus.

4. Biochemical Reactions

Important biochemical tests used for identification include the following:

Test Importance / Result
Catalase test Positive — useful for differentiating staphylococci from streptococci.
Coagulase test Used particularly to identify coagulase-producing staphylococci, especially S. aureus.
Mannitol fermentation Useful in differentiation; S. aureus is commonly associated with mannitol fermentation.
DNase May be used as an additional identification test.
Oxidase Generally negative in staphylococci.
Urease May be variable among species.

Coagulase test

The notes describe two approaches: slide coagulase test and tube coagulase test.

Exam point: Coagulase testing is especially important when S. aureus is suspected.

5. Pathogenesis

The notes describe two broad mechanisms of staphylococcal disease:

Infection
→
Intoxication

Infection

Infection can occur when staphylococci enter through damaged skin, mucosa or tissue.

After entering the host, the organism can attach to cells and multiply, leading to tissue damage and inflammation.

Important virulence factors

  • Cell-associated factors.
  • Cell-associated proteins.
  • Clumping factor.
  • Toxins.

6. Toxin-mediated Disease

Enterotoxins

Staphylococcal enterotoxins are associated with food poisoning and gastrointestinal symptoms.

Toxic shock syndrome toxin

The notes mention toxic shock syndrome as an important toxin-mediated disease.

Exfoliative toxin

Exfoliative toxin is associated with separation of superficial layers of the skin and can produce scalded skin syndrome.

7. Diseases Caused by Staphylococci

Folliculitis Infection involving hair follicles.
Furuncle A painful pus-forming skin infection.
Abscess Localized collection of pus.
Wound infection Infection can develop at damaged tissue or wound sites.
Osteomyelitis Infection involving bone.
Arthritis Joint infection may occur.
Pneumonia Respiratory infection involving the lungs.
Meningitis Infection involving the central nervous system.
Septicemia Severe systemic infection.
Endocarditis Infection involving the endocardium/heart.
Food poisoning Toxin-mediated illness.
Toxic shock syndrome Important toxin-mediated disease.
Scalded skin syndrome Associated with exfoliative toxin.

8. Laboratory Diagnosis

1. Specimen collection

The specimen depends on the site and type of infection. The notes mention examples such as wound material, pus and nasal swabs depending on the clinical situation.

2. Microscopy

A Gram-stained smear can demonstrate Gram-positive cocci arranged in clusters.

3. Culture

The specimen is cultured on suitable laboratory media. Colony appearance, pigmentation and haemolysis can provide useful clues.
