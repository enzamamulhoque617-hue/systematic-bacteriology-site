1. Morphology

  • N. meningitidis is a Gram-negative diplococcus.
  • The cocci occur characteristically in pairs.
  • The adjacent sides may appear flattened, giving a characteristic diplococcal appearance.
  • The organism possesses a capsule.
  • It is non-motile.
  • It is non-spore-forming.
Remember: Meningococcus → Gram-negative diplococci + capsule.

2. Cultural Characteristics

N. meningitidis is a fastidious organism requiring suitable enriched culture conditions.

Growth

  • Growth is favoured at approximately 35–37°C.
  • Growth is enhanced in an atmosphere containing carbon dioxide.
  • Enriched media are used for cultivation.

Culture media

Blood agar and chocolate agar may be used. Selective media can also be used when isolation from mixed flora is required.

High-yield: Meningococcus is fastidious and requires enriched culture conditions.

4. Pathogenesis

N. meningitidis may colonize the upper respiratory tract and can subsequently invade the bloodstream and central nervous system.

Nasopharyngeal colonization

The organism may colonize the nasopharynx. Attachment to mucosal epithelial cells is an important early step.

Invasion of bloodstream

The organism can enter the bloodstream, producing meningococcemia.

Role of capsule

The capsule is an important virulence factor and helps the organism resist phagocytosis.

Central nervous system

From the bloodstream, the organism may reach the central nervous system and produce meningitis.

5. Diseases Caused

Meningitis Important infection of the meninges caused by meningococcus.
Meningococcemia Bloodstream infection caused by invasive meningococci.
Septicemia Severe systemic infection may occur.
Petechial / purpuric rash A characteristic clinical finding may occur during meningococcal disease.
Fulminant infection Severe invasive disease can progress rapidly.

6. Laboratory Diagnosis

1. Specimen

Important specimens include cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and blood, depending on the clinical presentation.

2. Microscopy

Gram staining may demonstrate Gram-negative diplococci in an appropriate clinical specimen.

3. Culture

Suitable enriched media are used for isolation of the organism.

4. Biochemical identification

Oxidase and carbohydrate fermentation reactions can help identify the organism.

5. Molecular / antigen detection

Molecular and antigen-based methods can assist diagnosis, particularly when rapid diagnosis is required.

7. High-Yield Points

  • Gram-negative diplococci.
  • Encapsulated organism.
  • Non-motile and non-spore-forming.
  • Fastidious organism.
  • Oxidase positive.
  • Catalase positive.
  • Ferments glucose and maltose.
  • Causes meningitis and meningococcemia.
  • CSF and blood are important clinical specimens in invasive disease.
